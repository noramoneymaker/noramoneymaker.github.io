What it checks, and the exact thresholds

These are the Netflix-style caption rules that broadcasters, streamers and platforms reject on. They are the same six numbers the store's own delivery spec is measured against, read out of the same source file:

Overlapping or out-of-order cues and a broken numbering sequence are reported separately. A file that parses to zero cues is reported as an error rather than as clean — a checker that says "pass" on input it could not read is worse than no checker.

Fix it here, free

Flagging is the cheap half. Fix it and download rewrites the file you loaded into one that scores clean against the same six numbers, and hands it back as .srt and .vtt. All of it happens in this page — nothing is uploaded, and the rewriter is scored by the same checkSrt() that the $0.02 endpoint sells, so the score you see is the score the file gets.

It repairs, and counts what it repaired:

  • line breaks — rewrapped at word boundaries to 42 characters, two lines. A cue whose text needs more than two lines is split into two cues with its time divided between them, not truncated.
  • cue durations — clamped to the 7.0 s ceiling and floored at 0.84 s.
  • gaps — widened to the two-frame minimum, by shortening the previous cue's tail where it has slack and delaying the next start only where it does not.
  • numbering and order — renumbered from 1 and sorted by start time.

It does not change a single word. Not one is added, cut or re-spelled — only the whitespace inside a cue moves. That is deliberate: a rewriter that quietly edits speech is a transcription tool, and this one is not pretending to be.

Two things it will not repair, and names instead of hiding: a cue whose text cannot fit two lines inside the time it has (that needs retiming, which is an editorial decision) and reading speed over 20 characters a second (the only ways down are retiming or cutting words). Every cue it could not fix is listed under the file. A clean-looking file with a footnote nobody read is worse than an honest one.
